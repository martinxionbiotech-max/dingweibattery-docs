# CCA Testing

CCA (cold cranking amps) is the current a battery can deliver for 30 seconds at a defined cold temperature while maintaining a minimum cut-off voltage. It is the most widely compared battery metric — and the most often compared incorrectly.

## What CCA Measures

CCA answers one question: **will this battery start the engine in cold weather?** A higher CCA means more current is available to the starter motor at low temperature, where engine oil is thicker and cranking demand is highest.

## Why the Test Temperature Matters

CCA is **not** a single universal test. The main standards measure at different temperatures:

| Standard | Test Temperature | Common Use |
|---|---|---|
| JIS | −15°C | Japanese / Asian-market batteries |
| EN | −18°C | European batteries |
| SAE | −18°C | North American batteries |
| DIN | −18°C | German / European batteries |

Because JIS tests at a warmer −15°C, the same battery can show a different CCA number under JIS than under EN/SAE. The two are not interchangeable, and there is no universal percentage conversion.

## How to Compare CCA Correctly

1. **Confirm the test basis first** — JIS, EN, SAE, or DIN. Ask which standard the number refers to.
2. **Compare within one standard** — only compare JIS with JIS, EN with EN.
3. **Match the cut-off voltage** — some standards use different end voltages, which shifts the result.
4. **Treat the number as a reference** — the final CCA for a specific battery is confirmed by the manufacturer against its test procedure.

## CCA vs Capacity (Ah)

Ah (amp-hours) measures stored energy over time — how long the battery can supply current. CCA measures the peak current burst for starting. A vehicle with high electrical accessories needs Ah; a cold-weather diesel needs CCA. Most OEM specifications list both.
